# Writing the situated deck: the writer's brief

You are writing texture cards for **Dashiell**, a camp-noir deduction game in the shape of a bound book: New York's Lower East Side, the 1920s, one night, a detective ("I") who narrates. Every page is **locked facts** (testimony, a watcher's list, a find, the detective's reasoning) with **gaps** between them: texture. How he sits down, how he asks, how they take it, a line of the street, the page's last word.

Today each gap is filled by a generic card keyed by its slot alone. You are writing **situated** cards: each one written for the logical moment it sits in, so it fits its page. Each card is keyed by its gap's **situation**, and it will be dealt on *other* pages with the same situation.

## Read first (the voice)

1. `docs/golden/seed3-camp.md`: the voice. Camp, plain words, about one joke a page, the mystery played straight.
2. `docs/golden/board-connective.md`: what a page is made of, beat by beat.
3. the round-3 digest (`round3-digest.md`, from docs/47's playtests; the lead's scratchpad): four blind playtesters. Their **Keeps** lines are what works; their complaints (signposts, board-reading lines, place names bolted on, running gags overused) are what doesn't.

## Your batch

Your batch file lists about 300 gaps. Each gap shows:

- **its key** (the situation):
  - **slot**: what the gap does (below);
  - **page**: the kind of page: `office` (midnight, the client), `arrival` (walking into a place), `return` (back to the office), `account` ("where were you tonight?"), `admission-check` (asking the witness an admission named), `list` (a watcher: "who came in tonight?"), `search` (going over a room), `put` (putting the line that breaks their story to them), `motive` (how they felt about the dead man or the lost thing), `turn` (a chapter break: going over what I have), `report` (sitting down to the form at the end), `recap`;
  - **place**: `bar`, `club` (a speakeasy, a cellar club), `restaurant`, `theatre` (a theater or picture house: spell it "theater" in your text), `rooming` (a rooming house), `flat`, `rooms` (rented rooms over a shop), `home`, `work` and `shift` (a workplace at night: a bakery, a telephone exchange, a newspaper desk, a surgery), `unwatched` (a place nobody keeps the door of: a card room, an Automat, a back room), `scene-murder` (where the dead man was found), `scene-lost` (the flat the lost thing or pet went from), `office` (my office), `street` (a walk);
  - **manner**: the speaker's: `plain`, `terse` (few words), `careful` (slow, exact); `none` for the narrator's own slots;
  - **tier**: `low` (the book helps most), `mid`, `high` (it helps least, and says less);
  - **before / after**: what the nearest fact either side of the gap did: `new` (a fact nothing held touched), `confirms` (agreed with a line already held), `collides` (broke against a line already held), `narrows` (narrowed the hour, or the field), `none` (no fact on that side);
  - **put**: on put pages, how it went: `held` (it didn't touch their story), `admitted` (they owned up to being elsewhere), `refused` (they won't say), `landed` (they gave a second story), `cracked` (it all came out); `none` elsewhere;
  - **position**: the gap's paragraph: `first`, `middle`, `last`.
- **the page so far**, as the reader has it (paragraphs joined by ¶);
- what stays said **just before** your card (a why-here's or a hand-off's reason) or **right after** it (a first look at the person, put on after the staging): your card sits between them;
- **the next locked unit** (the fact after the gap), or that the page ends;
- **whom the tokens mean** and which tokens there are;
- **the card it had**: don't copy it, don't paraphrase it.

Write **one card for every gap**.

## The rule that matters most: write for the key, not the page

Your card will be dealt on other pages with the same key: the same slot, page kind, place kind, manner, tier band, before and after, position. The page you see is one example of that situation. So:

- Use what the key guarantees: the kind of place, the kind of page, the speaker's manner, what the fact before or after did.
- Never use a detail only this page has: no prop, no person's look or habit, no object or job named on this page, unless the place kind guarantees it (a bar has a bar and a barman; a theater has seats and an usher; a rooming house has a hall, stairs, a landlady; a dead man's flat has the body's absence of breath...). Don't name the lost thing's kind (a dog, a watch) unless you flag the case type (below).
- It has to read right before **any** next unit the key allows, and after any page that fits it.

## Slot by slot

- **why-here**: right after the reason I came, which stays ("Coffin’s list had Ashby here at eleven."). Your card turns the reason into going, or into asking: a short beat of resolve, attitude, or the place's welcome. Never restate the reason, name anyone or anywhere, or say "next" or "worth the walk". On `report` and `return` pages there's no reason before it: why I'm sitting down with the form, or what the office is like to come back to.
- **walk**: one line of the street at night between two places. The frame before it has said where and how far; you don't. The city at night, the cold, a cat, a streetcar bell, a shuttered newsstand, the hour without naming it.
- **staging**: where I sit or stand and how I open, before the question. On `search` pages, how I go over the room. On `report` pages, where I sit with the form. On `turn` pages, stopping to go over what I have. In the `office`, the client and the chair. A first look at the person may be kept right after your card: if so, don't describe them yourself.
- **ask**: the question, in my words: reported ("I asked {them} …") or spoken, in curly quotes. It asks exactly what the page is for:
  - `account`, `admission-check`: where they were tonight, the whole evening, and who with;
  - `list`: who came in tonight, and when (they keep the door, the bar, the house);
  - `motive`: what they thought of the dead man, or the lost thing, or the client: their reason;
  - `office`: with **after: narrows**, when it happened (or when they last saw it, him, her); with **after: new**, who would want to, who had a reason.
  Never say why I'm asking, never name anyone.
- **reaction**: how they take the question, before they answer. **Never a tell**: the same for a liar and an honest man. By manner: plain is straightforward, terse is few words, careful is slow and exact. On `put` pages it's how they take the line that breaks their story, by the put's outcome, without saying what they'll say.
- **close**: the page's last word. A small observation of the room, of me, of the night. Plain mostly. This is where the page's one joke usually lives.
- **gloss**: my aside right after a fact in my thinking, with no fact in it. After `confirms`, something about a story that holds; after `new`, taking it down as said; after `collides`, two stories; after `narrows`, the night getting smaller. **Never a verdict**: not "that left", "only one", "cleared", "guilty", "must have", not how many are left, not who's lying.
- **hand-off**: right after the reason for what I want next, which stays. A short turn toward it. No "next", no "worth the walk".

## The rules (a card that breaks one is cut)

1. One or two sentences, **8–40 words**. It opens a sentence (a capital, or an opening quote) and ends one (. ! ?).
2. **No names** of anyone or anywhere, **no hours** (seven to twelve, midnight, noon, o’clock, half past, "at two"), **no digits**, no honorifics, no "nobody else", no means words (knife, gun, revolver, pistol, poison, chloral, laudanum, arsenic, razor...).
3. **Never a bare he, she, him, her, his or hers.** The person the page is about goes through the tokens: `{they}` becomes he or she, `{them}` him or her, `{their}` his or her (a token that opens a sentence is capitalized for you). `{role}` is their role ("the landlady", "my client", "the dead man", "the dentist"). `{place}` is the place's short name ("Moe’s", "the Thalia"). `{thing}` is the lost thing or the pet's name (it may be "the brooch" or "Duchess": write so both read). Avoid `{means}`. Use only the tokens the gap lists. Most cards need none. "I" is the detective.
4. Don't copy or closely paraphrase the card it had, or any line of the game you've read.
5. **No facts.** Never say where anyone was, who saw whom, what happened, what's gone, who's lying, whether a story holds (beyond the gloss's attitude). No signposts ("next", "worth the walk", "which brought me to", "the one I’d come for", "So, Moe’s."). No game talk (the report wants, the board, clues, lines, stars). No "On the way out". No dawn and no morning: the night runs from midnight to eight.
6. **American spelling**: theater, neighbor, color, parlor, favor.
7. **Jokes: at most one card in ten.** A joke is a punchline or a figure meant to be funny; mark it `"joke": true`. Put them mostly on closes. The rest are plain: a concrete, specific, quiet line. Camp is timing and plain words, not density.
8. **Flags**, only where they apply:
   - `"requires": ["quote-before"]`: the card answers or reacts to speech said just before it;
   - `"requires": ["woman"]` or `["man"]`: only when the content is gendered past the tokens (a hatpin, a shave);
   - `"requires": ["lost-pet"]`, `["lost-item"]` or `["murder"]`: the card only fits that case (a dog's cushion, a dead man). A `scene-lost` page may be a pet or a thing;
   - `"requires": ["rain"]`, `["fog"]`, `["cold"]` or `["clear"]`: the card says the weather. Otherwise don't say it;
   - `"requires": ["leaving"]`: the card has me leaving the room (only the office's close is followed by leaving); `"forbids": ["leaving"]`: the card has me settling in.

## The voice

Plain words. Concrete things. The detective is a lovable wreck: deadpan about big things, melodramatic about small ones (coffee, chairs, rent, his feet, his coat). People are their types a notch louder, with affection: he never jeers at them. A death is never the joke.

What the testers kept:
- "Bellucci answered without looking at me, which is a way of answering."
- "I wiped my feet, late. It didn’t do much for me, but the stairs appreciated it."
- "One hand came unfolded, did something small, and went back. I’ve seen banks spend less carefully."
- "His glasses caught the light and did nothing else with it."
- "He looked at the floor for a while, as if it had been the one to tell me."
- "…put the key back after, which is manners of a kind."

Vary your cards within a key: different openings, lengths and angles. Not every staging starts "I sat", not every ask "I asked". Short ones are welcome.

## What else is on the page after your card

The batch shows the next **locked** unit; the gaps between your card and it aren't shown, but they're there, each with a card of its own:

- a **why-here** on an `account`, `admission-check`, `list` or `motive` page is followed by a staging (where I sit or stand), the ask (the question) and a reaction, then the answer. On a `put` page, by a staging, the line I put to them, and their reaction. On a `search` page, by the staging (how I go over the room) and the client's reaction, then the find. So a why-here there never sits me down, never asks, never starts the search: it is only the beat after the reason;
- a **staging** is followed by the ask and a reaction (on a `search` page, the reaction and the find);
- an **ask** is followed by the reaction, then the answer;
- a **why-here** on an `arrival` page comes after the place and the people in it, and is followed by my thought and the close.

## Notes from the batches before yours

- The checker counts sentences the way the book does: `“Where?” I said. “And who with?”` is three (the question mark ends one). Keep a spoken card to two: `“Where were you,” I said, “and who with?”`.
- Don't wink at the place in every card. A theater card with a cue, a program and a curtain call in a row reads as a bit; one in four is plenty, and the rest can be plain.
- Short cards (8–12 words) and long ones (25–40) are both wanted: the page's rhythm trades between lengths.
- The check also compares your cards with the batches written before yours, in the same (slot, page, before): a card too like one of theirs fails as a near-duplicate.
- A page in a key may have said anything a page like it can say: don't assume I ordered a drink, that the clock was knocked over, that a window won't latch. Keep to what every page of the key has.
- A room with a dead man in it stays quiet: no joke near the death, nothing that hints at how it was done. (The dead may be a woman now: "the dead man" only with `requires: ["murder"]` and only where {role} isn't available; better, "the dead" or nothing.)
- The verdict lint catches ordinary phrasings: "the one who", "took it", "was the one", "must have", "had to be", "cleared". Avoid them.
- "margin" and a few other words trip the book's machinery list; the check will say.
- `{they}` becomes he or she, so its verb is singular: `{they} was`, `{they} has`, `{they} doesn't`, `{they}’s`. `{they} were` comes out "he were" (only "as if {they} were" is right). The check catches were/are/have/do after it; watch for the rest ("{they} say" is "he say").
- Never the office as a source ("the office's version", "it squared with the office", "the office had it right"): that's the board read aloud, which the testers hated. And a card doesn't say again what the fact before it just said.
