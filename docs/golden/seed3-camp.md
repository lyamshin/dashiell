# Golden: camp, seed 3 at Medium

*The designer, 2026-09-23: "This isn't Sam & Max, but it doesn't hurt to pull that way. It's a game!" Then: "Maybe this describes a general issue. This is camp. We are making camp."*

*This golden sets the voice for everything after it. It covers the same case and facts as `seed3-people.md` (the office, arriving where the client is, asking a question, a recap), written as camp. Case facts come only from the case. Everything else is the detective's affection for a genre he knows he's in.*

## What camp means here

- **The mystery is played straight.** The logic is fair, the clues are true, and a dead man is not a punchline.
- **Everything around it is turned up, with love.** People are their types a notch louder. The weather is personal. The neighbourhood has opinions.
- **The detective is a lovable wreck.** He's melodramatic about small things (coffee, knuckles, rent, chairs) and deadpan about big ones.
- **Knowing, not winking.** The genre is honoured by being overdone on purpose. A simile can be too much, and it lands as a joke: one per page. The narrator never says "like in the pictures."
- **Plain words.** Camp isn't jargon. Every joke lands for someone who has never read Hammett, and every fact is clear on first read.
- **Timing, not density.** Short setups, clean punchlines, white space. Not more words per fact; more personality per beat.

## 1. The office

> Midnight. My office was a room at the top of a walk-up on Mulberry Street, and the radiator had decided that tonight it would be a sculpture. Frost had got in on the inside of the window. My knuckle had split open again in the same place as last month, which was at least consistent of it.
>
> A woman came up the stairs after midnight. Nobody comes up those stairs after midnight with good news. The good news waits for morning, like a gentleman.
>
> She had new gloves with the price tag still tucked in one cuff. She was in her thirties and dressed for an office she didn't have.
>
> "My name is Ilse Hauck," she said. "My sister was married to Isidore Sirkin. He's dead."
>
> I offered her the chair. It's the good chair, which tells you about the other one.
>
> "Sirkin was a buildings inspector," she said. "He could close a building with a signature. He'd closed two. Nobody on this street is going to send flowers." They found him in his walk-up. Crowninshield found him at half past eleven. She rents from him. Rented. "The police wrote it down as a fall and went home."
>
> "You don't think he fell."
>
> "The door was locked. The windows are painted shut. There's one key, it hangs on a hook at the third floor, and this morning it was on the hook." She folded the gloves. "The coroner says between eight and half past. My brother-in-law did not fall down between eight and half past and then lock the door behind himself."
>
> It was a fair point. I've fallen down plenty, and I've never once remembered to lock up afterwards.
>
> "Why me?"
>
> "Because the police are finished with it, and I would like it to stay finished in public." She traded stocks on the street for men who would rather not be seen doing it. Quiet was her line of work. "Find out who. Then forget you did. I'm paying for both."
>
> "Where would you start?"
>
> "Steinbach. He blamed Sirkin for losing his business. Loudly. To anyone."
>
> She put a hundred dollars on the desk and kept two fingers on it until I picked it up. I respected that. I'd have done the same.

## 2. Walking in on the client

> The speakeasy was six steps down under a hat shop, through a door you had to knock on. The knock is a formality. They let in anybody who knows about the door, which is everybody.
>
> Most of the stools were empty on account of the cold. Hargrove was behind the bar rinsing glasses in a basin, a big man going soft, the kind of bartender who knows what you drink before you do and never once says your name out loud.
>
> Hauck had a table at the back. She moved her gloves off the other chair, which in her language was a warm welcome.
>
> "Sit down," she said. "I'll tell you who's who."
>
> "That's what I'm paying you for," I said. "Wait. Other way round."
>
> "The woman with the newspaper is Crowninshield. The dentist. She found him." She didn't look over. "The man at the end of the bar comes in here. Couldn't tell you his name. The girl two stools down from him, I've never seen in my life. And Hargrove knows everybody's drink and nobody's business, which is why he's still alive."
>
> I looked the room over the way she'd laid it out. The dentist who found the body was reading a newspaper at one in the morning without turning the page. Either it was a very good paragraph or she wasn't reading it.

*The rundown is free, once a visit. Hauck names people only the way she knows them. The last line is Dashiell's own observation, tying someone present to the case.*

## 3. The ask

> I took the stool next to Crowninshield. She was a dentist of about forty-one, and she had the look of a woman who had bought her practice second-hand and was still paying off the chair. She was also writing something on a little pad without looking at it, which is a skill, or a nervous habit, or both.
>
> I asked her where she'd been tonight. I asked it nicely. I even offered to buy her a drink, which in that room was mostly a gesture.
>
> "Here," she said. "From eight until half past nine. Before that I was on the third floor, at half past seven. Before that the subway kiosk, at seven." She tore the page off the pad and folded it very small. "I like to know where I've been. Somebody should."
>
> I wrote it down. That was Crowninshield's evening, in Crowninshield's words, and so far nobody else's. A dentist tells you it won't hurt. You nod, and you check later.

*The approach is on a stool. The look at her is drawn from her dossier (bought the practice second-hand, still paying for the chair) and from what she's doing (her recall action, the pad). The ask is in reported speech, the try is a drink, and the answer is her account, in her words. The wry close is one line, and it isn't a verdict.*

## 4. The recap

*After Hauck gives up her secret on the second fact.*

> I went back to my corner and took stock, which is the part of the job where you find out how little you have.
>
> Sirkin died in his walk-up between eight and half past. The whistle off the river went at half past eight, and so far it was the only honest clock on the street. The coroner said chloral, so somebody poured him a drink he didn't finish.
>
> Hauck had told me the third floor, then the office, and now the truth: she'd been right here, selling things that weren't hers to sell. Hargrove could say whether that was so, and I hadn't asked him. Crowninshield had her evening and nobody's word under it but her own. Vitale's I had from Rafferty, with a hole in it at eight you could drive a milk wagon through. Marchetti I had only seen at the bar. Nobody had told me where she was at half past eight, including Marchetti.
>
> That was the night so far: a lot of people who were somewhere, and one hour nobody wanted to talk about. I'd ask Hargrove next. Bartenders are the only people in this city who get paid to remember.

*Every clause is a notebook fact, or the absence of one. The jokes stay on the people and the job, never on the facts. There's no verdict.*

## Rules for the voice

1. **Straight mystery, camp world.** The logic and the deaths are serious. Everything else is fair game.
2. **One joke that lands beats three that don't.** Aim for at most one real punchline per paragraph, and let a page breathe between them.
3. **The detective is the butt of his own jokes first.** He punches at the night, the rent and himself. He never jeers at the people in a way that makes them smaller than the story needs them to be.
4. **Types, turned up.** Every person's trade and habit is a little louder than life, and still a person.
5. **Plain words, always.** If a joke needs a glossary, cut it.
6. **Facts are never the setup of a gag that blurs them.** "A hole in it at eight you could drive a milk wagon through" keeps "a hole at eight" exact.
7. **Figures are jokes now.** A simile or a flourish should be funny or it goes. One per page, as before.
