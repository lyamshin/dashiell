# Golden: the night, seed 3

*Pages 2 to 5 of seed 3 at Precinct, written by hand. The office opening has its own golden, `seed3-opening.md`. This one covers what comes after it: arriving somewhere, searching, walking into a room with people in it, and asking a question.*

*Every fact here is in the case's data: the truth sheet, the clues found on these pages, the dossiers, the places, the weather. The texture is invented, as texture decks are. Nothing that matters to the case is invented.*

---

## Page 2 · the suite · 12:25 AM

*Go to the suite. First arrival at the scene; the scene report and the coroner's note come free.*

> *Kreuzer said they found Sweeney in his suite. I wanted to see the room before anybody tidied it.*
>
> The residential hotel was brick, six floors of it, the kind of place where the rent is paid by the week and nobody minds who uses the stairs. Sweeney had kept the suite on the fourth floor. The door was shut but not locked. The precinct had taken its statement and gone home.
>
> Sweeney was still on the floor where he had fallen. The lamp had come down with him. I held my hand near the bulb. It was still warm, and it had not broken.
>
> The coroner's man had left a note on the back of an intake form. Between half past nine and eleven. One blow to the back of the skull, and it had not killed him at once.
>
> The El ran past the window, close enough to hit with a thrown shoe. A train had gone over at ten o'clock, on time, and for twenty seconds under that structure nobody hears anything at all.
>
> Kreuzer had given me two hours. The El might give me the minute. If it happened at ten, it happened under the train, and a whole building could have missed it.

**Why it works.**

1. **The errand opens the page, and the page carries it through.** The detective came to see the room, so the page is the room. The last paragraph answers the errand: what did the room tell me?
2. **The place is established before anything happens in it.** Say what the place is and what kind of place it is, and give one detail of how it's run: "nobody minds who uses the stairs" is how an unwatched place reads. Then say whose it is. One paragraph, on the first visit only.
3. **Who is here?** Nobody alive. That's said plainly, and the body is acknowledged, because it is there. The current engine said "Nobody in here but the furniture" with a dead man on the rug.
4. **The finds are dramatized in the order a person would notice them,** each with its source. The coroner's words come from the coroner's note. The El comes from the window.
5. **The detective thinks.** The last paragraph is a thought, and it is derived: the coroner's window, set against an anchor that falls inside it. This is the "when" of the case, and the page says so without saying "anchor."
6. **One figure,** the thrown shoe. No others.

## Page 3 · the suite · 12:50 AM

*Search the room. One find: the rug and the chair (t001), which opens "Kreuzer on Hanrahan."*

> *The precinct had looked at Sweeney. I wanted to look at the room.*
>
> I started at the door and worked in. The rug was rucked up under Sweeney in one long fold, the way a rug goes when two people are standing on it and one of them pushes. The chair beside him had gone over backwards.
>
> A day ledger lay on the desk. There was a nickel-plated revolver in the room as well. I left them both where they were for now.
>
> Nothing had been carried out. The drawers were shut and there were no gaps on the shelves.
>
> So it was a fight, and it was not a robbery. Whoever came up those stairs came for Sweeney and nothing else.
>
> A man in Sweeney's line keeps a secretary, and a secretary knows who has an appointment. His was a woman named Hanrahan. Kreuzer would know where Hanrahan had been tonight, and Kreuzer had told me where she would be.

**Why it works.**

1. **The errand carries into a second page at the same place.** It isn't only for arrivals. Every action gets its reason, one line, in the detective's voice.
2. **The objects the buttons offer are named in the story.** The ledger and the revolver are search choices. Here they're mentioned and deliberately left, so the buttons read as "go back to that," not as a menu.
3. **The find and the thought are two different paragraphs.** The rug is what the room shows. "It was a fight, and it was not a robbery" is what the detective makes of it. That conclusion is exactly what the clue establishes ("nothing was carried out"), said as a conclusion.
4. **The bridge.** The find opened a lead: ask Kreuzer about Hanrahan. The page says why that is the next question, in terms the reader already has: Hanrahan's relation to the victim. That's what makes the next errand line feel earned instead of arbitrary.

## Page 4 · the speakeasy · 1:15 AM

*Go to the speakeasy. Present: Kreuzer (the client) and Callahan (the bartender, who watches the place).*

> *Sweeney's secretary would know who came to the suite. Kreuzer would know where the secretary had been.*
>
> The speakeasy was under the hat shop, six steps down and through a door you had to knock on. By day the street knew it as a place to buy a hat. After midnight it was a long room with a low ceiling and a bar down one side, and most of the stools were empty on account of the cold.
>
> Callahan was behind the bar, a woman in her forties with her sleeves pushed up, putting clean glasses back on the shelf one at a time. She looked at me once, the way a bartender looks at everybody, and went back to the glasses.
>
> Kreuzer was at a table at the back with a cup of coffee she had not touched. The coin was going over her knuckles again. When she saw me she put it away.
>
> If anybody had come or gone tonight, Callahan would know it, and Callahan would decide whether I did. I didn't mind that Kreuzer was here. I minded a little that she had picked the one table with a view of the door.

**Why it works.**

1. **The place is established the same way as the suite:** what it is, how you get in, what it is at this hour, and how the weather has emptied it.
2. **Everyone in the room is doing something.** Each person gets one activity, drawn from their trade, the place, and the hour. The bartender puts away glasses. The client waits with a coffee. It's said once and not repeated on the next page unless it changes.
3. **The client's tic comes back once, as recall,** not as an epithet. "The coin was going over her knuckles again" is the payoff of page one. The current engine calls her "the woman with the unlooked-at coin flip" four times.
4. **Everyone gets a name and a reason to matter.** Callahan is the bartender, and bartenders watch the room. That's derived from the place's watcher, and it tells the reader why the bartender is worth a question.
5. **The detective has an opinion about the people.** It's small and in character, and it's the kind of thing a reader keeps.

## Page 5 · the speakeasy · 1:40 AM

*Ask Kreuzer about Hanrahan. One find: c015, "Kreuzer says Hanrahan was at the third floor from 10:00 PM to 10:30 PM." It's an observation, so Kreuzer was there to see it. The truth agrees, and so would Kreuzer's own account if anyone asked her evening. Nobody has yet, and she didn't volunteer it in the office.*

> I sat down across from her. "Sweeney had a secretary. Hanrahan."
>
> "Nora Hanrahan. She's been with him since 'eighteen."
>
> "Where was she tonight?"
>
> "At the walk-up on Ninth. From ten until half past." Kreuzer turned the coin over once on the table. "I saw her there."
>
> I wrote it down. Then I looked at what I'd written.
>
> If Hanrahan was on Ninth Street at ten, she wasn't in the suite when the El went over. That was good news for Hanrahan. But Kreuzer had seen her there with her own eyes, which put Kreuzer on Ninth Street at ten o'clock too. She had sat at my desk for half an hour and never mentioned it.
>
> I didn't ask her about it. Not yet. A client who leaves things out is still a client, and she was paying.

**Why it works.**

1. **The question has a reason, and the reason is the last page's bridge.** No separate errand line is needed when the question itself says why. The asking is the errand.
2. **The answer is in the witness's mouth, in her register.** The clue's sentence, "Kreuzer says Hanrahan was at the third floor from 10:00 PM to 10:30 PM," becomes something Kreuzer says. The place is given the way a local would say it ("the walk-up on Ninth"), and the time the way a person would say it.
3. **The thought is where the game is.** Two conclusions come from one clue, both derived:
   - A placement at the crime's half hour clears Hanrahan, if it's true.
   - An observation places the observer. The client was out on Ninth Street at the hour of the murder, and she left it out of her briefing.

   This is the detective doing the reader's work out loud, once, clearly. The engine has every piece of it: the clue's kind (observation), its source (Kreuzer), its time (the crime's half hour), and what the office page did and did not say.
4. **The page ends on a decision, not a sign-off.** The current engine closes with "Don't leave town on my account," said to the client who hired him.

## The rules

These come from the four pages above and add to the ten rules of the opening golden.

1. **Every action page opens with its reason.** Moves get the errand line. Searches and questions at the same place get one line of reason too, unless the question itself carries it.
2. **The page answers its errand.** Whatever the detective came for, the last paragraph says what came of it: found, not found, or found something else.
3. **The first visit establishes the place.** What it is, how you get in, what it is at this hour, how the weather has emptied or filled it, and whose it is. One paragraph. A return visit gets one line.
4. **Everyone present is named and is doing something.** One activity each, from their trade, the place and the hour. It's said once and kept for the visit; the next page mentions it only if it changes. A person's recall phrase appears at most once per visit and is never used as an epithet.
5. **A person gets a reason to matter on first sight.** Watchers watch. The client is the client. Someone the notebook already knows gets one clause of who they are to the case.
6. **Find, then thought.** What the world shows and what the detective makes of it are separate paragraphs. The thought is derived from the clue's kind and the notebook: a placement clears or implicates, an observation places the observer, a contradiction is named as one, a motive is named as one, a dead end is called a dead end.
7. **When a find opens a lead, the page names it and says why,** in terms the reader already has: the subject's relation to the victim, to the place, or to the time.
8. **Nothing leaks across pages.** The client's goodbye belongs to the office page. Texture about the hour matches the clock. No "four in the morning" at 2:05.
9. **No unexplained names.** Anyone named gets one clause saying who they are, the first time they are named on a page.
10. **The thought and the bridge are never cut.** When a page is long, texture goes first.
