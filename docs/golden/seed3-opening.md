# Golden example — seed 3, the opening (v2)

*Written by hand against the generated facts of seed 3 (Kreuzer, Sweeney, Grasso; the Lower East Side; a cold night; Dashiell behind on the rent with a split knuckle). This is the target the engine is measured against. It is not a template. It is what a good page reads like.*

*Stylist's brief, revised: the goal is competent, enjoyable pastiche, not replication. Hammett at full density reads poorly now. Clarity comes first. A sentence the reader has to parse twice is cut. Plain sentences carry the page, and because there are few words, every plain sentence has to be exact. The client talks in whole turns; Dashiell asks short questions that make sense as questions. Description follows one thing at a time and stops. One figure per two pages at most, and the page must survive its removal.*

---

## Page one — the office

Midnight. Two rooms over a Chinese laundry on Rivington Street. The laundry's steam warmed the floor all day and quit at six. By midnight the cold was back in the room, and I was at the desk not opening the rent envelope.

Someone started up the stairs. One person, slow, in heels.

She knocked once and came in without waiting for an answer. She was about thirty, in a good coat that had belonged to someone else first. She kept her left hand in the pocket until she sat down. When she took it out I saw why: the little finger had been broken once and never set.

"Mr. Dashiell."

"Sit down."

She sat. She kept the coat on.

"My name is Gretchen Kreuzer. I write the tickets at Feldman's pawnshop on Orchard Street. I know what things are worth." She said it like a reference. "Martin Sweeney is dead. You know the name."

I knew it. Sweeney rented the top floor of the residential hotel on Rivington and supplied liquor to four bars on the block. The bars stayed open because he did.

"I found him," she said. "Half past eleven, in his rooms. Somebody hit him with something heavy, more than once. The police came, took my statement at the front desk, and went home."

"What were you doing in his rooms at half past eleven?"

"Collecting." She let that sit. "He owed me money. He still does. That is why I am here instead of in bed."

"So you want the man who killed him."

"I want what I am owed. If the man who killed him has my money, then yes, I want him too. Start with Grasso. He works the pier when there is work. Sweeney ruined him two years ago, and Grasso has said so in every bar on the street."

She put a roll of bills on the desk, held with a rubber band. I did not count it. She watched me not count it.

"Twenty dollars a day," I said. "I tell you what I find, not what you want to hear."

"That will do."

## Page two — the suite

The suite was on the fourth floor at the back of the hotel. The night clerk let me up without asking my name, which told me the police had already been, and that someone had paid him to stop caring.

The door was unlocked. Nobody had cleaned up. A lamp lay on the rug with its shade off and the bulb unbroken. The bed had not been slept in. On the sill of the open window, a cigarette had burned itself down to the paper and gone out.

They had taken Sweeney to the morgue. The rug still showed where he had been.

I stood in the doorway and looked. The window was open, four floors up, with the latch thrown back. That was the one thing in the room that did not belong.

The coroner's note lay on the table under a glass, left there for whoever came next. Death between half past nine and eleven. One blow to the back of the skull, perhaps more. He had not died quickly.

At ten o'clock every night the El goes over the end of the block. For twenty seconds you cannot hear anything under it. I wrote that down and went back downstairs to find the night clerk.

## Professor's notes: what changed from v1, and why

Every edit below is a clarity edit. None of them changes a fact.

| v1 | v2 | why |
|---|---|---|
| "by midnight the cold had the place back" | "By midnight the cold was back in the room" | "Had the place back" is a flourish the reader has to decode. Say the plain thing. |
| "doing nothing about the rent when the stairs started" | "not opening the rent envelope. Someone started up the stairs." | "The stairs started" is elliptical. Two plain sentences read faster than one clever one. |
| "One person, taking them slow. A woman, by the heels." | "One person, slow, in heels." | The reader had to infer that heels are a sound. Now it's a detail, not a riddle. |
| "set by nobody" | "never set" | Stylized negation. The plain form is stronger. |
| "the liquor for four houses on the block, and the four houses stayed open because of it" | "supplied liquor to four bars on the block. The bars stayed open because he did." | "Houses" is period slang the reader can't be expected to know. Pastiche uses period detail the reader can follow. |
| "The precinct came" | "The police came" | Same reason. "Precinct" is fine as a place; as a metonym for the officers it's a step removed. |
| "If the man who did it is where the money went, then yes." | "If the man who killed him has my money, then yes, I want him too." | The v1 line had to be parsed twice. This is the same idea in the order a person would say it. |
| "Sweeney broke him" | "Sweeney ruined him" | "Broke" is ambiguous in a murder story. |
| "Sweeney had gone to the morgue, but the floor remembered where he had been." | "They had taken Sweeney to the morgue. The rug still showed where he had been." | The figure was doing the plain sentence's job. Now the plain sentence does it, and the image is the fact. |
| "I stood in the doorway and let the room tell me what it wanted to. It wanted to tell me about the window." | "I stood in the doorway and looked. The window was open, four floors up, with the latch thrown back. That was the one thing in the room that did not belong." | The v1 was mannered: a room with wants. The point is that the window is the anomaly. Say so. |
| "the night man" | "the night clerk" | Names the job. |

**What was kept, and why.** "She said it like a reference." (one small figure, instantly clear). "Collecting." (one word that answers the question and characterizes her). "I did not count it. She watched me not count it." (the pair is the only rhythmic trick on the page and it is legible on first read). The El at ten o'clock (evidence stated as a fact of the block, which is how the whole game should handle time).

## What the golden does, as rules (revised)

1. **Clarity first.** A sentence the reader has to parse twice is cut or split. This rule outranks the others.
2. **One scene, one line of attention.** The office page is about the woman. The suite page is about the room.
3. **The client carries the exposition in speech, and Dashiell's questions are real questions.** Each of his lines asks for the fact the next turn gives. If a question could precede any answer, it is not a question, it is a prod.
4. **Description is sequential and stops.** Coat, hand, finger. Lamp, bed, sill. One item per plain sentence, three items, then move.
5. **Period detail, not period slang.** Feldman's pawnshop, the El, a rubber band around a roll of bills. Never a word the reader needs a glossary for.
6. **One figure per two pages, and the page survives without it.**
7. **Cohesion by reference.** Every paragraph's first sentence carries a noun or pronoun from the paragraph before it.
8. **Rhythm from sentence length, not from tricks.** Mostly short and plain; one longer sentence per page that carries information, not ornament.
9. **Time is a fact of the world.** "At ten o'clock every night the El goes over." Never a clock reading as a mechanic.
10. **Dashiell has a body and a mood in one sentence each, then gets out of the way.**
