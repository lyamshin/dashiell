# Golden: people, seed 3 at Medium

*Three moments from seed 3 at Medium, Precinct, written by hand after the designer's read (2026-09-23):*

> This still reads way too thin. Character cards should carry more info. Someone shouldn't talk about themself as a mystery half the time. The order of these things is wrong. There should be a free action when you visit where the client is to give you a rundown of who is there, who they know at least. But overall it reads poorly.

*Case facts come only from the case: the dossiers, the client brief, and the acquaintance graph. Lines marked ◆ are **character material**. It's the kind of thing a richer archetype card supplies: habits, how the person talks, how the street sees them. It's true of the type and asserts nothing about the case.*

---

## 1. The office, in order

**Today the page runs:** her trade → "Isidore Sirkin is dead" → the locked door and the key → the coroner and who found him → the precinct → "I am his sister-in-law" → Dashiell asks "What happens if it is settled loudly?" before she has said anything about quiet → a suspect → money.

**The order a person tells it in:** who I am to him → that he's dead → where, and who found him → what the police made of it → what the door and the key say → when → why I've come to you and not them → what I want → who I'd start with → the money.

> Midnight. Two rooms over a Chinese laundry on Mulberry Street. My knuckle had split open again, the same place as last month, and frost had crept across the window glass from the inside. A woman came up the stairs after midnight.
>
> She stood a moment inside the door with her gloves in one hand. They were new enough that a price tag was still folded down inside one cuff. She hadn't noticed, or hadn't cared to. She was in her thirties, dressed for an office she didn't have. ◆ She had the look of someone who did her business standing up, on a curb, with one eye on the street.
>
> "My name is Ilse Hauck," she said. "My sister was married to Isidore Sirkin. He's dead."
>
> I let her sit before I asked anything.
>
> "Sirkin was a buildings inspector," she said. "He could close a building with a signature, and he had closed two. You can imagine how many friends that made him. They found him in his walk-up. Crowninshield found him, at half past eleven. She rents from him."
>
> "And the police?"
>
> "They wrote it down as a fall and closed the book on it." Her mouth went thin. "The door was locked. The windows were painted shut. There's one key to that walk-up, and it hangs on a hook at the third floor, and this morning it was on the hook. The coroner says between eight and half past. That's not a fall. That's somebody with a key."
>
> "Why me, and not the precinct again?"
>
> "Because the precinct is finished with it, and I'd like it to stay finished in public." She turned the gloves over. "I want it found. I don't want it said. I'm paying for both."
>
> ◆ She traded stocks on the street for men who would rather not be seen doing it themselves. She knew what quiet was worth to a customer, and she was pricing it for herself.
>
> "Where would you start?"
>
> "Steinbach. He blamed Sirkin for losing his business. He said so to anyone who'd listen."
>
> She put a hundred dollars on the desk and waited for me to pick it up before she let go of the edge of it.

**Why it works.**

1. **Relation first.** Who she is to the dead man is the first thing out of her mouth, because it's why she's there.
2. **The story in the order it happened to her:** dead → where → who found him → what the police did → why that's wrong → when.
3. **The detective asks only what her last line invited.** "Why me?" comes after she has said the precinct closed it. There are no questions about things not yet said.
4. **Character carries information.** "Traded stocks on the street for men who would rather not be seen" is her profession and her motive in one line, and it sets up her "keep it quiet".
5. **The victim gets a sentence of life and consequence:** "You can imagine how many friends that made him."

## 2. Arriving where the client is: the rundown

**Today:** a half hour buys a page where the bartender rinses glasses and three people are listed. There's nothing to do on the page itself.

**New rule:** when the client is in the room, the arrival page ends with a free choice, **"Ask Hauck who's here."** The rundown is the client naming whoever she knows, the way she knows them. It costs nothing, once per visit. She knows Crowninshield "by relation" and Vitale "by sight", and doesn't know Marchetti. The bartender everybody knows by trade.

> I came down the six steps and knocked, and the door let me in.
>
> The speakeasy was a long low room under a hat shop, a bar down one side, and on a night this cold most of the stools were empty. Hargrove was behind the bar rinsing glasses in a basin. ◆ He was a big man going soft, the kind of bartender who knew what you drank before you did and never said your name out loud.
>
> Hauck had a table near the back. When she saw me she moved her gloves off the other chair.
>
> "You found me," she said. "Sit down. I'll tell you who you're looking at."
>
> "Go on."
>
> "The woman with the newspaper is Crowninshield. The dentist. She's the one who found him." She didn't look over. "The man at the end of the bar I've seen in here. I couldn't tell you his name. The girl two stools down from him, I don't know at all. And Hargrove you know, or you will. He knows everybody's drink and nobody's business."
>
> I looked the room over the way she'd laid it out. The dentist who found the body was reading a newspaper at one in the morning without turning the page.

**Why it works.**

1. **The client earns her fee as a guide.** Only her acquaintance edges are used: a relation, a face known by sight, a stranger.
2. **Strangers stay strangers.** She gives no names she doesn't know, so the grid's description entries still need linking.
3. **One observation of Dashiell's own closes the page.** It's drawn from the person's activity ("reading a newspaper without turning the page") and their tie to the case ("who found the body"). A page that cost a half hour gives something to think about.

## 3. A person asked about themselves

**Today:** "I am 45 years old and the landlady. I keep the third floor and sit where I can see the stairs. I am the keeper of the third floor." That's the generator's record read aloud. And the question is "Who are you when nobody is asking?", which frames her as a mystery.

**New rule:** the question is plain ("How long have you had the house?", "What's your line?", "How did you know Sirkin?"). The answer is the person talking about their life in their register. It draws on their dossier (trade, detail, tie, since, want), in first person, the way people say such things. The **enigma** temper means someone *guarded*: short answers, and they don't volunteer. They still answer about themselves plainly. Only when asked about the night do they hold things back.

> "How long have you had the house?" I said.
>
> Rafferty put the coins down in their stacks. "Eleven years this spring. My husband bought it and I've kept it since he died, which is longer than he had it." ◆ "I sit where I can see the stairs. People think that's nosiness. It's rent."
>
> "You knew Sirkin?"
>
> "Everybody knew Sirkin. He came round twice a year about the stairs and the fire escape, and twice a year I paid him not to find anything." She said it the way you'd mention the weather. "That's not a secret on this street. It's the price of a stair."

*(Rafferty's "paid him not to find anything" is ◆ character material about how a buildings inspector and a landlady get along. It's true of the type, and it gives her no motive the case doesn't hold. A card like this may only speak about the victim in the victim's own trade terms, never assert a case fact.)*

**Why it works.**

1. **A life, not a form.** The dossier's facts (landlady, keeps the third floor, sits where she can see the stairs) are all there, spoken by a person.
2. **Relation to the victim, in her words.** It tells the reader who these people were to each other.
3. **She isn't a mystery.** Guardedness belongs to the night, not to a person's own life.

---

## The rules

1. **The office runs in the order a person tells it.** Relation, death, where and who found him, the police, what's wrong with their story, when, why me, what I want, who first, money. Dashiell asks only about what was just said.
2. **Meeting someone gives a character, not two facts.** On first sight, three to five sentences: what they look like and what they're doing, their trade, and one line of how the street sees them (◆), plus their tie to the case if the detective knows it. It's drawn from layer 0 and from archetype character cards.
3. **Asked about themselves, people talk about their lives.** First person, their register, built from their dossier. No "I am 45 years old and…". No questions that frame them as a mystery.
4. **Enigma means guarded, not mysterious.** Guarded people answer about themselves plainly and briefly. They hold back only about the night. Roll enigma for at most one person in a case.
5. **When the client is in the room, "Ask <client> who's here" is free, once a visit.** The client names people by their acquaintance edges and admits strangers are strangers.
6. **An arrival page gives something to think about.** It closes on one observation of Dashiell's own that ties someone present to the case.
7. **Archetype character cards (◆)** carry habits, speech, the street's view of a type, and how the type deals with the victim's type. They're true of the type, never a case fact, and plain words only.
