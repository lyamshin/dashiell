## Page 5 · the third floor · 1:35 AM

*Ask Rafferty (the landlady, who watches the third floor) about the key. Facts: only one key to the walk-up, kept on Ninth, and Marchetti had it off the hook that evening. A stranger sighting: a woman under forty at the third floor at ten.*

> *Rafferty ran the house. If a key had gone anywhere tonight, she'd know where.*
>
> Rafferty finished the stack she was on before she looked up. She wrote the total on the envelope in pencil, and only then did I have her attention.
>
> "The walk-up on Ninth," I said. "Who has a key to it?"
>
> "There's one key. There's only ever been the one." She said it the way you say something you've had to tell the gas company twice. "It lives on a hook there. Tonight it wasn't on the hook. Marchetti had it."
>
> "You're sure it was Marchetti?"
>
> "It's my business to be sure who has my keys."
>
> I wrote it down. One key, and Marchetti had it. That didn't make Marchetti anything yet. It made her somebody who could open a door.
>
> "Anybody else through here tonight that you didn't know?"
>
> Rafferty touched the string at her collar where her own keys hung. "A woman. Ten o'clock. Under forty, I'd say. I didn't know her, and she didn't stop to be known."
>
> A woman under forty, at ten, on a stair she didn't live on. She went into the book without a name.

## Page 6 · the third floor · 2:00 AM

*Ask Rafferty about Vitale, Sirkin's creditor. Facts: Rafferty saw Vitale at the third floor from seven until half past, and at nine; not there the rest of the evening. Rafferty knows him by name.*

> Sirkin had owed money to a man named Vitale. I asked Rafferty if she knew him.
>
> "Carmine Vitale. Everybody on this block knows Carmine Vitale." She didn't say it kindly. "He was up here at seven. Gone by half past. Back at nine, and not for long."
>
> "And the rest of the evening?"
>
> "Not here. I'd have heard him. He takes the stairs like each one owes him money."
>
> So Vitale had not been on the third floor at eight. That was one place he wasn't. It left a good many places he might have been.

## Page 7 · the third floor · 2:25 AM

*Ask Rafferty about the third floor. Facts: head counts (one at half past six, one at half past eight, two at nine, one at half past nine); at half past ten, nobody but Ilse Hauck; strangers: a woman under forty at half past six, a man in his thirties at half past seven.*

> "Who came up those stairs tonight?" I said. "All of it."
>
> Rafferty turned the rent envelope over, as if the back of it were a ledger. "Quiet night. One at half past six. One at half past eight. Two at nine, and one at half past nine. At half past ten it was Ilse Hauck, and nobody with her."
>
> "And the ones you didn't know?"
>
> "A woman under forty at half past six. A man in his thirties at half past seven. I don't learn names off the backs of people's coats."
>
> I copied it into the notebook the way she'd said it. A head count doesn't tell you who. It tells you how many chairs there were at the table, and somebody had been sitting in one of them at half past eight.

## The rules

1. **One question, one telling.** Facts of one kind (a person's comings and goings, a place's head counts, a stranger's sightings) arrive together in a single natural answer, the way a person would say them. Never one question per fact.
2. **The witness says it in their own words.** A fact is never the generator's rule sentence in quotation marks. Times are spoken, places are given the local way ("the walk-up on Ninth"), and people are named the way this witness knows them.
3. **Every telling gets a grounding.** That's a clause or line that makes it believable: how the witness knows ("It's my business to be sure who has my keys"), what they were doing, or why they'd notice. The grounding comes from their trade, their post and their acquaintance, and states no case fact beyond what the fact itself gives.
4. **A follow-up where it's natural.** "You're sure it was Marchetti?" or "And the rest of the evening?" It breaks a fact into a conversation and gives the second half of the fact its own line.
5. **A tail that isn't a fact.** Opinion, attitude, a small joke: "He takes the stairs like each one owes him money." It gives the witness a character, and it's the only place a figure of speech goes.
6. **The detective's note is short and says what the fact is worth, not what it proves.** One or two sentences: "That didn't make Marchetti anything yet. It made her somebody who could open a door." There's no rule restated and no verdict.
7. **Pages breathe.** At most three kinds of fact are told on one page. When a witness has more, the page ends with a free "Go on" choice that continues the conversation without costing a half hour.
8. **Prose around facts is plain first.** Flavor is at most one line in a telling, and never jargon.
